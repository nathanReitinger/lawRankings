Subject areas

Specialty journal, group, higher-level group

Every specialty journal sits in one group, and every group in one higher-level group: thirteen subject areas, the journals of the interdisciplinary methods, and one higher-level group of general and policy journals. Each journal's place rests on its own statement of what it publishes and on its W&L subject; each group rests on its member journals' own words; both are cited below. The higher-level groups are also the buckets authors go in: a person's tags on the rankings page are the higher-level groups of the specialty journals they published in. Nothing is guessed from titles.

How it was built

The thirteen subject areas come from how law professors divide their own field, checked against library and research classifications; two more higher-level groups hold the journals defined by a method (Interdisciplinary Methods) and the journals of law or policy generally (General & Policy Journals). The groups inside them were built from the journals up (section 4). Each specialty journal was placed by its own scope statement and its W&L subject, in a fixed table. A person's tags are read off that table: nothing about a person is inferred.

1. How professors divide their own field

Fourteen schemes that law professors or law faculties wrote down: Brian Leiter's most-cited-by-specialty lists, Jotwell's sections, the U.S. News specialty list, the "areas of study" pages at Harvard, NYU, Columbia, Stanford, Berkeley and Georgetown, Peter Hook's map of which courses the same teacher teaches, the field coding in Bonica, Chilton, Rozema and Sen (2018), and the areas new law teachers list together in Sarah Lawsky's hiring data (1,645 hires, 2011–2026). Two more (Michigan's faculty areas and the Federalist Society practice groups) were seen only in search snippets and are marked unverified. Law professors' blog posts filled in how the field divides in hiring and in citations: Sarah Lawsky's entry-level hiring reports on PrawfsBlawg (2025 and 2026), her posts on hiring committees' topics and the make-your-own report, two Faculty Lounge posts (Tim Zinnecker on the most-listed hiring areas, Alfred Brophy on trusts and estates beside tax), Michael Dorf on public law and the "business side" (Dorf on Law, 2007), Randy Barnett on the Volokh Conspiracy (2005), and Roger Alford on field size and citations (Opinio Juris, 2007). A field became an area if it stands alone in at least 7 of the 14 schemes. That rule gives the thirteen subject areas: constitutional law and criminal law stand alone in all 14, health law in 7.

2. Library and research classifications

The subject areas and their earlier subdivisions were matched node by node against 11 classification schemes: the Library of Congress classification for law, SSRN's Legal Scholarship Network (an archived copy), the AALS subject codes, the Australian and New Zealand research classification, the economists' JEL codes, the bepress Digital Commons disciplines, Jotwell, Leiter's lists, the West Key Number System's top headings, and OpenAlex topics (machine-made, for contrast only). These schemes agree closely on fields (roughly 20 to 25 recognizable units) and much less on how to group them into a handful of areas, which is why the professors' own groupings set the top level. Each of the thirteen subject areas in the tree above lists the scheme nodes it matches.

3. How journals are placed

Each specialty journal was first placed in one field (the subdivisions that the groups of section 4 later replaced, inside the same higher-level groups), from two things only: the journal's own statement of what it publishes (its name counts as part of that statement) and its subject in the W&L Law Journal Rankings. Nothing about the papers it has published is used. The rule, applied in order: a statement that names one subject decides the field; a statement that names several goes by the one it names first or as central, with the W&L subject breaking a tie; a journal with no usable statement goes by its W&L subject; a journal whose statement covers law, or law and public policy, generally goes to "General & Policy Journals", which counts in the rankings but gives no one an area; and a journal that defines a method or discipline (empirical work, economics, history) goes to the matching Interdisciplinary Methods field.

Two placers applied the rule independently, each without seeing the other's answers or any earlier placement, and agreed on 225 of 231 journals; a third decided the other 6 by the same rule. For 113 journals the scope statement was taken from the journal's own page (or its publisher's), for 67 from a search result's quotation of it, for 13 from an encyclopedia description of the journal (its Wikipedia article), and 38 had none on file (placed by name or W&L subject). Each journal's deciding words are recorded with the data. Compared with the earlier placement, which also used the share of each journal's faculty papers in each field (judged from titles), 27 journals changed area and 57 changed field within their area. Journals W&L ranks later take the group of their W&L subject until they are placed the same way.

4. How the groups were made

The groups were built from the journals up, inside the same higher-level groups (2026-10-26). Three designers worked independently, each from the journals' own scope statements, names and W&L subjects only: one clustering by the subjects the statements name, one from how law schools divide teaching and scholarship, one for groups a reader can tell apart and of similar size. A judge chose the best design for each higher-level group and took clearly better names or splits from the others. Then two placers who had not seen the design re-placed all 231 journals from the group definitions alone: both put every journal where the design did (231 of 231). Each group is defined in one to three sentences; the smallest also say why they stand alone. Most hold 2 to 12 journals (International law, with 18, is the largest); ten hold a single journal whose subject is distinct from the rest of its higher-level group (education law and immigration law, for example). Each group's definition and its members' own words are shown under the tree.

5. How authors are placed in buckets

Each higher-level group is a bucket of authors: the current faculty with an article in one of its journals. On the rankings page, a person's tags are the higher-level groups of the specialty journals they have published in, each with the number of their articles there, all years. Someone with articles in two areas' journals gets two tags. Someone who has published only in flagship law reviews gets none: a flagship article says nothing about a subject here. Opening a person shows the same thing as a small tree, higher-level group, then group, then the journals, for the years chosen. No paper title is read to decide any of it.

What was merged, split or moved, and why

  • Trusts & estates join Tax rather than Property, in the higher-level group Tax, Trusts & Estates (the group Trusts & estates: the ACTEC Law Journal): trusts-and-estates scholars publish with tax scholars about six times more often than chance, Hook's teaching map has a "Taxation/Wealth Preservation" cluster, and the AALS codes cross-reference the two. The Real Property, Trust and Estate Law Journal names real property first, so it sits in Real property law.
  • Property joins Environment and Natural Resources: property stands alone in only 5 of the 14 schemes, its most frequent partner in the hiring data is environmental law, and so is its strongest publishing link after the constitutional-law hub. Property, Environment & Natural Resources holds four groups: Environmental, energy & natural resources law, American Indian law, Animal law and Real property law.
  • Family law joins Civil Rights, Equality & Family: family, gender and antidiscrimination scholars form one publishing community, and NYU and Columbia both use "Family, Gender & Sexuality". Family law (the Family Court Review and the Family Law Quarterly) sits beside Gender, sexuality & feminist legal studies.
  • Trial advocacy sits in Criminal Law & Justice, as a group of its own (the American Journal of Trial Advocacy, whose W&L subjects span civil litigation and criminal law): evidence and trial scholars publish (narrowly) more with criminal-law than with civil-procedure scholars, and new teachers list criminal procedure and evidence together. No collected journal centres on evidence.
  • Elder law joins Health Law & Bioethics (the group Elder law: the Elder Law Journal): elder-law, disability and mental-health scholars publish most with health-law scholars. No collected journal centres on disability or mental health.
  • Immigration sits in Civil Rights, Equality & Family (the group Immigration law: the Georgetown Immigration Law Journal), the closest call in the tree: new law teachers list immigration with race and civil rights, and the Library of Congress files citizenship beside civil and political rights. The human-rights journals sit in Human rights law, under International & Comparative Law.
  • Education law sits in Civil Rights, Equality & Family (the Journal of Law & Education) and is kept apart from legal education, the teaching of law (the Clinical Law Review and the Journal of Legal Education), which sits in Legal Profession & Education.
  • Science sits with technology in Science, Technology & IP (the group Law, science & technology, seven journals), following the Australian and New Zealand classification, bepress and W&L's own subject list. Journals of law and medicine sit in Health law, medicine & bioethics.
  • Intellectual property sits under Science, Technology & IP (the group Intellectual property, eleven journals): IP and technology journals share the same authors in every test (NYU's "Antitrust, IP & Information", Georgetown's "Technology, Communication, and IP"). Arts, entertainment & sports law sits here too, as in the research tree: some of its journals name intellectual property or technology among their topics (Cardozo, Vanderbilt).
  • Privacy has no group of its own: no collected specialty journal centres on it; the technology journals that list privacy among their topics (the Harvard Journal of Law & Technology, Florida's Journal of Technology Law & Policy, the Duke Law & Technology Review) sit in Technology law and in Internet, computing & communications law, under Science, Technology & IP.
  • American Indian law sits in Property, Environment & Natural Resources (the American Indian Law Journal and the American Indian Law Review): Indian-law scholars publish little in other fields, the nearest area by both measures is this one, and no outside scheme groups Indian law with another field.
  • Tort law sits in Courts, Litigation & Dispute Resolution (the Journal of Tort Law), as in the hiring data and Leiter's "Torts and Insurance" list, in a group of its own: torts is substantive private law, not courts or procedure.
  • Antitrust stays in Business & Financial Law, in Antitrust & consumer protection law with the Loyola Consumer Law Review: the law of market conduct, kept apart from the law of the firm and of finance.
  • Method journals have their own higher-level group, Interdisciplinary Methods (2026-10-26): the fifteen journals whose statements name a method or discipline rather than a subject (Law & economics, Law & psychology, Empirical & socio-legal studies, Legal history), much as SSRN and the economists' JEL codes file subjects under methods. An article in one of them tags its author with Interdisciplinary Methods, not with a subject area.

Known limits

  • Journals, not titles. A person who writes about, say, privacy only in flagship law reviews shows no tag for it; only specialty journals speak to a subject.
  • Thin venue coverage for some subjects: no collected specialty journal centres on law and religion or on privacy, and legal history rests on a single journal (the American Journal of Legal History), so those subjects are thin or absent here.
  • Law and religion has no group. The Library of Congress and AALS schemes file church and state under constitutional law, so the subject belongs with Constitutional & Public Law, but no collected journal puts it there; its scholars are tagged by the other journals they publish in.
  • One group per journal. A journal whose scope spans two subjects (law and medicine, say) counts toward the one its statement names first.
  • A statement is only as good as its wording. A journal that describes itself in general terms lands in "General & Policy Journals" even when its pages lean one way; one with no statement on file goes by its W&L subject or name.
  • Current faculty only: older years show today's faculty's early work, not the field as it was.
  • Close calls are close. Immigration, science, trial advocacy, antitrust, education law, family law and American Indian law could each defensibly sit in another area.
  • One snapshot. Counts come from the data of ; they shift as the data grow.
