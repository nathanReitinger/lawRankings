Subject areas

Specialty journal, group, higher-level group

Each specialty journal is placed in one group, and each group in one higher-level group: thirteen subject areas, the journals of the interdisciplinary methods, and one higher-level group of general and policy journals. A journal's placement rests on its own statement of what it publishes and on its W&L subject; a group rests on its member journals' own words; both are cited below. The higher-level groups are also the author buckets: a person's tags on the rankings page are the higher-level groups of the specialty journals they published in. Paper titles are not used.

How it was built

The thirteen subject areas come from how law professors divide their own field, checked against library and research classifications; two more higher-level groups hold the journals defined by a method (Interdisciplinary Methods) and the journals of law or policy generally (General & Policy Journals). The groups inside them were built from the journals up (subsection 4 below). Each specialty journal was placed by its own scope statement and its W&L subject, in a fixed table. A person's tags are read from that table alone.

1. How professors divide their own field

Fourteen schemes that law professors or law faculties wrote down: Brian Leiter's most-cited-by-specialty lists, Jotwell's sections, the U.S. News specialty list, the "areas of study" pages at Harvard, NYU, Columbia, Stanford, Berkeley and Georgetown, Peter Hook's map of which courses the same teacher teaches, the field coding in Bonica, Chilton, Rozema and Sen (2018), and the areas new law teachers list together in Sarah Lawsky's hiring data (1,645 hires, 2011–2026). Two more (Michigan's faculty areas and the Federalist Society practice groups) were seen only in search snippets and are marked unverified. Law professors' blog posts filled in how the field divides in hiring and in citations: Sarah Lawsky's entry-level hiring reports on PrawfsBlawg (2025 and 2026), her posts on hiring committees' topics and the make-your-own report, two Faculty Lounge posts (Tim Zinnecker on the most-listed hiring areas, Alfred Brophy on trusts and estates beside tax), Michael Dorf on public law and the "business side" (Dorf on Law, 2007), Randy Barnett on the Volokh Conspiracy (2005), and Roger Alford on field size and citations (Opinio Juris, 2007). A field became an area if it stands alone in at least 7 of the 14 schemes. That rule gives the thirteen subject areas: constitutional law and criminal law stand alone in 14 of 14, health law in 7.

2. Library and research classifications

The subject areas and their earlier subdivisions were matched node by node against 11 classification schemes: the Library of Congress classification for law, SSRN's Legal Scholarship Network (an archived copy), the AALS subject codes, the Australian and New Zealand research classification, the economists' JEL codes, the bepress Digital Commons disciplines, Jotwell, Leiter's lists, the West Key Number System's top headings, and OpenAlex topics (machine-made, for contrast only). Roughly 20 to 25 fields recur across these schemes; the schemes that group fields into broader areas do so in different ways. The top level here follows the professors' own groupings (subsection 1 above). Each of the thirteen subject areas in the tree above lists the scheme nodes it matches.

3. How journals are placed

Each specialty journal was first placed in one field (the subdivisions that the groups of subsection 4 later replaced, inside the same higher-level groups), from two things only: the journal's own statement of what it publishes (its name counts as part of that statement) and its subject in the W&L Law Journal Rankings. The papers it has published are not used. The rule, applied in order: a statement that names one subject decides the field; a statement that names several goes by the one it names first or as central, with the W&L subject breaking a tie; a journal with no usable statement goes by its W&L subject; a journal whose statement covers law, or law and public policy, generally goes to "General & Policy Journals", which counts in the rankings and adds no area tag to its authors; and a journal that defines a method or discipline (empirical work, economics, history) goes to the matching Interdisciplinary Methods field.

Two placers applied the rule independently, each without seeing the other's answers or any earlier placement, and agreed on 225 of 231 journals; a third decided the other 6 by the same rule. For 113 journals the scope statement was taken from the journal's own page (or its publisher's), for 67 from a search result's quotation of it, for 13 from an encyclopedia description of the journal (its Wikipedia article), and 38 had none on file (placed by name or W&L subject). Each journal's deciding words are recorded with the data. Compared with the earlier placement, which also used the share of each journal's faculty papers in each field (judged from titles), 27 journals changed area and 57 changed field within their area. Journals W&L ranks later take the group of their W&L subject until they are placed the same way.

4. How the groups were made

The groups were built from the journals up, inside the same higher-level groups (2026-10-26). Three designers worked independently, each from the journals' own scope statements, names and W&L subjects only: one clustering by the subjects the statements name, one from how law schools divide teaching and scholarship, one for groups a reader can tell apart and of similar size. A judge chose one design for each higher-level group and took names or splits from the others where the judge rated them higher. Then two placers who had not seen the design re-placed the 231 journals from the group definitions alone; each placer's placements matched the design for 231 of 231. Each group is defined in one to three sentences; the smallest also say why they stand alone. Most hold 2 to 12 journals (International law, with 18, is the largest); ten hold a single journal, defined apart from the rest of its higher-level group (education law and immigration law, for example). Each group's definition and its members' own words are shown under the tree.

5. How authors are placed in buckets

Each higher-level group is a bucket of authors: the current faculty with an article in one of its journals. On the rankings page, a person's tags are the higher-level groups of the specialty journals they have published in, each with the number of their articles there, across the years collected. A person with articles in two areas' journals has two tags. A person with articles only in flagship law reviews has no tag: flagship articles carry no subject here. Opening a person shows the same thing as a small tree, higher-level group, then group, then the journals, for the years chosen. Paper titles are not used.

Merges, splits and moves

  • Trusts & estates join Tax rather than Property, in the higher-level group Tax, Trusts & Estates (the group Trusts & estates: the ACTEC Law Journal). Basis: authors publishing in both fields (trusts and estates, and tax) at about six times the chance rate; the "Taxation/Wealth Preservation" cluster in Hook's teaching map; the AALS codes' cross-reference between the two. The Real Property, Trust and Estate Law Journal names real property first and sits in Real property law.
  • Property joins Environment and Natural Resources. Basis: property listed as a field of its own in 5 of the 14 schemes; environmental law as property's most frequent partner in the hiring data and its strongest publishing link after constitutional law. Property, Environment & Natural Resources holds four groups: Environmental, energy & natural resources law, American Indian law, Animal law and Real property law.
  • Family law joins Civil Rights, Equality & Family. Basis: family law, gender and antidiscrimination law in one cluster of fields by shared authors; the area "Family, Gender & Sexuality" at NYU and at Columbia. Family law (the Family Court Review and the Family Law Quarterly) sits beside Gender, sexuality & feminist legal studies.
  • Trial advocacy sits in Criminal Law & Justice, as a group of its own (the American Journal of Trial Advocacy, whose W&L subjects span civil litigation and criminal law). Basis: rates of authors publishing in both evidence or trials and criminal law, and in both evidence or trials and civil procedure (the first slightly higher); criminal procedure and evidence listed together by new law teachers. The collected journals do not include one centered on evidence.
  • Elder law joins Health Law & Bioethics (the group Elder law: the Elder Law Journal). Basis: rates of authors publishing in elder law, disability or mental health and also in another field, highest for health law. The collected journals do not include one centered on disability or mental health.
  • Immigration sits in Civil Rights, Equality & Family (the group Immigration law: the Georgetown Immigration Law Journal). Basis: immigration listed with race and civil rights by new law teachers; citizenship filed beside civil and political rights in the Library of Congress classification. The human-rights journals sit in Human rights law, under International & Comparative Law.
  • Education law sits in Civil Rights, Equality & Family (the Journal of Law & Education) and is kept apart from legal education, the teaching of law (the Clinical Law Review and the Journal of Legal Education), which sits in Legal Profession & Education.
  • Science sits with technology in Science, Technology & IP (the group Law, science & technology, seven journals), following the Australian and New Zealand classification, bepress and W&L's own subject list. Journals of law and medicine sit in Health law, medicine & bioethics.
  • Intellectual property sits under Science, Technology & IP (the group Intellectual property, eleven journals). Basis: authors shared between IP and technology journals in the tests run here; NYU's "Antitrust, IP & Information" and Georgetown's "Technology, Communication, and IP". Arts, entertainment & sports law sits here too, as in the research tree: some of its journals name intellectual property or technology among their topics (Cardozo, Vanderbilt).
  • Privacy has no group of its own: the collected specialty journals do not include one centered on it; the technology journals that list privacy among their topics (the Harvard Journal of Law & Technology, Florida's Journal of Technology Law & Policy, the Duke Law & Technology Review) sit in Technology law and in Internet, computing & communications law, under Science, Technology & IP.
  • American Indian law sits in Property, Environment & Natural Resources (the American Indian Law Journal and the American Indian Law Review). Basis: this area as the nearest to Indian law by both measures; the low share of Indian-law scholars' articles in other fields; Indian law not grouped with another field in the outside schemes collected here.
  • Tort law sits in Courts, Litigation & Dispute Resolution (the Journal of Tort Law), as in the hiring data and Leiter's "Torts and Insurance" list, in a group of its own (substantive private law, apart from courts and procedure).
  • Antitrust stays in Business & Financial Law, in Antitrust & consumer protection law with the Loyola Consumer Law Review: the law of market conduct, kept apart from the law of the firm and of finance.
  • Method journals have their own higher-level group, Interdisciplinary Methods (2026-10-26): the fifteen journals whose statements name a method or discipline rather than a subject (Law & economics, Law & psychology, Empirical & socio-legal studies, Legal history), much as SSRN and the economists' JEL codes file subjects under methods. An article in one of them tags its author with Interdisciplinary Methods, not with a subject area.

Known limits

  • Journals, not titles. A person whose articles on, for example, privacy appear only in flagship law reviews has no tag for it; tags come from specialty journals.
  • Journals per subject: the collected specialty journals do not include one centered on law and religion or on privacy, and legal history has a single journal (the American Journal of Legal History).
  • No group for law and religion. The Library of Congress and AALS schemes file church and state under constitutional law (Constitutional & Public Law here); the collected journals do not include one on the subject, and its scholars are tagged by the other journals they publish in.
  • One group per journal. A journal whose scope spans two subjects (law and medicine, say) counts toward the one its statement names first.
  • Placement by wording. A journal that describes itself in general terms is placed in "General & Policy Journals" whatever the subjects of its articles; one with no statement on file goes by its W&L subject or name.
  • Current faculty only: counts for earlier years are articles by today's faculty, not by the faculty of those years.
  • Alternative placements. Immigration, science, trial advocacy, antitrust, education law, family law and American Indian law each had a second candidate area; their placements are listed under "Merges, splits and moves".
  • One snapshot. Counts are from the data of and change when the data are updated.
