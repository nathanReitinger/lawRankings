Subject areas

From specialty journal to category

Every specialty journal sits in one field, and every field in one category: thirteen subject areas, the journals of the interdisciplinary methods, and a group of general and policy journals. Each journal's place rests on its own statement of what it publishes and on its W&L subject, and both are cited below. The categories are also the buckets authors go in: a person's categories on the rankings page are the categories of the specialty journals they published in. Nothing is guessed from titles.

How it was built

The categories (the subject areas) and their fields come from how law professors divide their own field, checked against library and research classifications. Each specialty journal was then placed in a field by its own scope statement and its W&L subject, in a fixed table. A person's categories are read off that table: nothing about a person is inferred.

1. How professors divide their own field

Fourteen schemes that law professors or law faculties wrote down: Brian Leiter's most-cited-by-specialty lists, Jotwell's sections, the U.S. News specialty list, the "areas of study" pages at Harvard, NYU, Columbia, Stanford, Berkeley and Georgetown, Peter Hook's map of which courses the same teacher teaches, the field coding in Bonica, Chilton, Rozema and Sen (2018), and the areas new law teachers list together in Sarah Lawsky's hiring data (1,645 hires, 2011–2026). Two more (Michigan's faculty areas and the Federalist Society practice groups) were seen only in search snippets and are marked unverified. A field became an area if it stands alone in at least 7 of the 14 schemes. That rule gives 13 areas: constitutional law and criminal law stand alone in all 14, health law in 7.

2. Library and research classifications

The areas and fields were matched node by node against 11 classification schemes: the Library of Congress classification for law, SSRN's Legal Scholarship Network (an archived copy), the AALS subject codes, the Australian and New Zealand research classification, the economists' JEL codes, the bepress Digital Commons disciplines, Jotwell, Leiter's lists, the West Key Number System's top headings, and OpenAlex topics (machine-made, for contrast only). These schemes agree closely on fields (roughly 20 to 25 recognizable units) and much less on how to group them into a handful of areas, which is why the professors' own groupings set the top level. Every area and field in the tree above lists the scheme nodes it matches.

3. How journals are placed

Each specialty journal sits in one field, from two things only: the journal's own statement of what it publishes (its name counts as part of that statement) and its subject in the W&L Law Journal Rankings. Nothing about the papers it has published is used. The rule, applied in order: a statement that names one subject decides the field; a statement that names several goes by the one it names first or as central, with the W&L subject breaking a tie; a journal with no usable statement goes by its W&L subject; a journal whose statement covers law, or law and public policy, generally goes to "General & Policy Journals", which counts in the rankings but gives no one an area; and a journal that defines a method or discipline (empirical work, economics, history) goes to the matching Interdisciplinary Methods field.

Two placers applied the rule independently, each without seeing the other's answers or any earlier placement, and agreed on 225 of 231 journals; a third decided the other 6 by the same rule. For 120 journals the scope statement was taken from the journal's own page, for 73 from a search result's quotation of it, and 38 had none on file (placed by name or W&L subject). Each journal's deciding words are recorded with the data. Compared with the earlier placement, which also used the share of each journal's faculty papers in each field (judged from titles), 27 journals changed area and 57 changed field within their area. Journals W&L ranks later take the field of their W&L subject until they are placed the same way.

4. How authors are placed in buckets

Each category is a bucket of authors: the current faculty with an article in one of its journals. On the rankings page, a person's tags are the categories of the specialty journals they have published in, each with the number of their articles there, all years. Someone with articles in two areas' journals gets two tags. Someone who has published only in flagship law reviews gets none: a flagship article says nothing about a field here. Opening a person shows the same thing as a small tree, area, then field, then the journals, for the years chosen. No paper title is read to decide any of it.

What was merged, split or moved, and why

  • Trusts & estates join Tax rather than Property: trusts-and-estates scholars publish with tax scholars about six times more often than chance, Hook's teaching map has a "Taxation/Wealth Preservation" cluster, and the AALS codes cross-reference the two.
  • Property joins Environment and Natural Resources: property stands alone in only 5 of the 14 schemes, its most frequent partner in the hiring data is environmental law, and so is its strongest publishing link after the constitutional-law hub.
  • Family law joins Civil Rights & Equality: family, gender and antidiscrimination scholars form one publishing community, and NYU and Columbia both use "Family, Gender & Sexuality". Cross-listed with Health through reproduction.
  • Evidence, juries and trials sit in Criminal Law: their scholars publish (narrowly) more with criminal-law than with civil-procedure scholars, and new teachers list criminal procedure and evidence together. Cross-listed under Courts.
  • Disability, mental health and elder law join Health, where their scholars publish most.
  • Law & religion sits in Constitutional & Public Law, as in the Library of Congress and AALS schemes; cross-listed under Legal Theory.
  • Immigration sits in Civil Rights & Equality, the closest call in the tree; cross-listed under International (human rights).
  • Science sits with technology in "Science, Technology & IP", following the Australian and New Zealand classification, bepress and W&L's own subject list; biotechnology and genetics are cross-listed under Health.
  • Intellectual property sits under Science, Technology & IP: IP and technology journals share the same authors in every test (NYU's "Antitrust, IP & Information", Georgetown's "Technology, Communication, and IP").
  • Privacy is its own field under Science, Technology & IP: data-protection regulation, government surveillance (cross-linked to the Fourth Amendment), health and genetic privacy, privacy theory and torts.
  • Federal Indian law sits in Property, Environment & Natural Resources, its nearest area by both measures; cross-listed under Civil Rights and International.
  • Legal education is kept apart from education law; torts and remedies sit in Courts, Litigation & Dispute Resolution; antitrust stays in Business, cross-listed under Science, Technology & IP.

Known limits

  • Journals, not titles. A person who writes about, say, privacy only in flagship law reviews shows no privacy tag; only specialty journals speak to a field.
  • Thin venue coverage for some fields: no collected specialty journal centres on jurisprudence, legal history or law and religion, so few people carry those fields.
  • One field per journal. A journal whose scope spans two fields (law and medicine, say) counts toward the one its statement names first.
  • A statement is only as good as its wording. A journal that describes itself in general terms lands in "General & Policy Journals" even when its pages lean one way; one with no statement on file goes by its W&L subject or name.
  • Current faculty only: older years show today's faculty's early work, not the field as it was.
  • Close calls are close. Immigration, science, law and religion, evidence and juries, antitrust, education law and family law could each defensibly sit in another area.
  • One snapshot. Counts come from the data of ; they shift as the data grow.
