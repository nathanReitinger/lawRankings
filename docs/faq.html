What is this site?

This site provides rankings and analytics on law school faculty scholarship output. This metric-based sorting of schools helps bring transparency to publications and helps connect communities of scholars. It is modeled on CSrankings.

How are rankings calculated?

Just as they are on CSrankings. Each article is one credit, split evenly between its co-authors (the adjusted count). Each area is either the flagship law reviews (all the tiers you check under Flagship Law Reviews, together) or one higher-level group of specialty journals (all the groups you check in it, together). A school's count is the geometric mean, across the selected areas, of its faculty's total adjusted count in that area plus one. Articles are credited to the school where the author teaches now, so a professor who moves brings their earlier articles along.

What do "Per professor" and "Leave out a school's own journals" do?

Two options under Options, both off by default; neither is the site's ranking. Per professor divides each school's count (less the one added to each area) by the number of people on its faculty list, so it asks how much a faculty publishes for its size rather than in all; with one area it is simply adjusted articles per listed professor. The list is HeinOnline's faculty list for the school, which can include clinical, emeritus and visiting faculty, and schools with fewer than ten people listed are left out. Leave out a school's own journals stops counting an article for the author's school when that school publishes the journal: its main law review, its online edition, or a specialty journal it hosts. Faculty publish in their own school's review far more often than other schools' faculty do, so this takes away a home advantage; the article still counts for a co-author at another school. In our check, each agrees with U.S. News a little less than the default does (see below).

What are "flagship" law reviews?

A school's main, general-interest law review, such as the Harvard Law Review or the Northwestern University Law Review. W&L lists these in its "General" category, together with online editions (the Yale Law Journal Forum, for example) and a few other general journals; in the rankings those form their own tier, "Online editions & other general journals", which is off by default. The other journals are specialty journals. Each sits in one of 52 groups, and each group in one higher-level group: thirteen subject areas, plus "Interdisciplinary Methods" for the method journals and "General & Policy Journals" for journals whose scope is law or policy generally. Each journal is placed by its own statement of its scope and its W&L subject; see Subject areas for the tree, how it was built and its sources. Open a faculty member on the rankings page to see bars for where they publish: one for flagship law reviews, one for online editions & other general journals, and one for each higher-level group they have published in, counting all their articles in the years you chose (without short pieces when "Skip short pieces" is on), with how many of them count in the current ranking. The site does not list individual articles; a name links to the person's own faculty page where we have it.

What do the tags next to a name mean?

The higher-level groups (subject areas) of the specialty journals that person has published in, each with the number of their articles there, over all years, and nothing else: each higher-level group is a bucket of authors. Each specialty journal sits in one group and each group in one higher-level group; the Subject areas page draws the line from every journal through its group to its higher-level group and cites what each placement rests on (the journal's own statement of its scope and its W&L subject), so the tags are read straight off the journals: no thresholds, and nothing is guessed from titles. Journals whose scope is law or policy generally make no tag. Articles in two areas' journals make two tags. Someone who has published only in flagship law reviews (where nearly every law professor publishes, whatever their field) has no tag. Open a faculty member to see "What they work on": the same thing as a small tree (higher-level group, group, journals) for the years you chose.

Some examples, as of October 2026: Mark Lemley (Stanford) is tagged Science, Technology & IP 31 (technology law 16, intellectual property 11, internet, computing & communications law 2 and two more: the Berkeley Technology Law Journal, the Harvard Journal of Law & Technology, the AIPLA Quarterly Journal and others), and also Business & Financial Law 2, Constitutional & Public Law 1 and Interdisciplinary Methods 1; Richard Revesz (NYU), Property, Environment & Natural Resources 18 (all environmental, energy & natural resources law, from the Environmental Law Reporter to the Harvard Environmental Law Review), Constitutional & Public Law 5 (administrative law & regulation) and Interdisciplinary Methods 5 (the Journal of Legal Studies); Stephen Henderson (Oklahoma), Criminal Law & Justice 9, Constitutional & Public Law 3 and Science, Technology & IP 1; Reuven Avi-Yonah (Michigan), Tax, Trusts & Estates 25, International & Comparative Law 9 and four more. A count of 1 is shown like any other: one article in a group's journal is a fact, and the count lets you weigh it. Each of them also publishes in flagship law reviews, which make no tag.

How can I spot a school or a person on the rise?

Each school and each faculty member in the ranking has a By year line (on screens wider than a phone): their counted articles in the selected journals in each selected year, oldest on the left, adjusted for co-authors and scaled to their own busiest year; hover it for the numbers. A line that is flat on the left and climbs on the right is a school or person that has only lately begun to publish in those journals. Open a school, or a person, for a line chart of the same counts over the twenty years up to the end of the selected range, or the whole range if you select more than twenty years (the selected years shaded), one line per selected group of journals (when more than five are selected, the smaller ones are folded into one line) and their total, so a recent rise shows against what came before. They follow the filters: change the journals, the years, short pieces or "Leave out a school's own journals" and the lines and charts change with them ("Per professor" changes only the ranking; the lines stay counts).

A school also has subject areas, read the same way as a person's tags: the higher-level groups of the specialty journals its current faculty published in during the selected years, each with its number of articles (an article two of its faculty wrote counts once). The top three show beside its name on wider screens; open it for all of them.

Beta: categories from titles

Off by default, and never used for the rankings or the tags unless you turn it on (Options, "Beta: categories from titles"). A model reads an article's title and guesses its higher-level group: TF-IDF word features and a logistic regression, trained on 181,196 titles of articles in 209 specialty journals, each labeled with its journal's higher-level group. Tested on journals it never saw (five folds, grouped by journal), it names the right one for 46% of titles (the right one is among its top three for 71%), against 18% for always guessing the largest. It does best for international law (72% of those titles) and science, technology & IP (64%), and worst for legal theory (2%), courts and litigation (20%) and labor (20%). With the switch on, a person's tags count every article in any journal, flagship law reviews included, whose title the model is at least 50% sure of, marked β. It shows what a title-based reading would look like; the site's own tags come only from the specialty journals.

How are articles matched to faculty?

By name, against HeinOnline's lists of faculty by school, where each person appears once under their HeinOnline author name. An article counts for a faculty member when its author, as the journal's table of contents lists it, has the same last and first name, with middle names or initials that do not conflict (the exact name first; then the first and last name when only one person on the lists has it; then the same ignoring a "Jr." or "III" one side leaves off). A name that belongs to two faculty members is credited to neither; an article whose author has a middle initial is not credited to a faculty member listed without one unless at least 85% of the articles under that name that give a middle initial give that one; and in magazines that are not a school's journal (the ABA Journal, for example), an author listed without a middle initial is not credited to a faculty member listed with one, since staff writers share many professors' names. A second pass then catches the other ways one person's name is written, each only when exactly one person on the lists fits: the same words with the comma elsewhere ("Reiser, Dana Brakman" for "Brakman Reiser, Dana"), an initial plus the name someone goes by ("R. George Wright" for Richard George Wright), initials only ("J. M. Balkin"), a title before the name ("Dean Matthew Diller"), and a string that lost its comma. Initials count only for papers from 1985 on and within ten years of the person's other work, since older ones were namesakes. A nickname ("Rich" for Richard), the middle name used as the first, or a middle initial the list leaves off counts only with evidence: a paper in a journal the person's own school publishes, or a co-author shared with their other papers. A name HeinOnline itself marks as a different person ("Gardner, James A. (I)") is not credited to the untagged name. A piece HeinOnline labels a student Note is not credited to a faculty member whose other credited work began five or more years before it, since a namesake student wrote it; an editor's, research or historical note is the faculty member's own, and so are pieces when HeinOnline labels several in one volume "Note". A credit 30 or more years before a person's next credited article is left out as a namesake's or a historical figure's, unless it is under the same spelling as their recent work. If something looks wrong, please suggest a correction.

How are journals ranked?

  • Flagship law reviews take their school's rank (), and come in tiers of that rank ("1–3", "4–25", "26–50", "51 and above"); schools tied at a rank share a tier, and reviews of unranked schools are in "51 and above". The school rank can be the U.S. News overall rank (the default) or the U.S. News peer-reputation rank, from the scores deans and faculty give each school (as published on TaxProf Blog); choose under "Flagship Law Reviews". The default view counts the main law reviews of the top 25.
  • Specialty journals take their rank in the Washington & Lee Law Journal Rankings (, combined score). They sit in groups and higher-level groups (see below); open a higher-level group in the filters to see its groups, and a group to see its journals in order of W&L combined score, each with its score and the school that publishes it with that school's rank. "Count each higher-level group's top N" counts the N highest-scoring journals across the groups you check in it (journals W&L does not score count only when "all" is chosen), and "from schools ranked" can limit the specialty journals to those published by schools in a rank range.

These ranks only decide which journals are counted. Schools themselves are ordered by the count described above.

Which years does the default view count?

Ten years of volumes, ending with the latest year whose volumes are mostly in (now ): the latest year in which current faculty's articles in the default journals (each co-author counted) reach at least 75% of their average over the five years before it. Journals post their issues over time, so a year that has only begun would pull every school down unevenly. An article's year is the last year in its volume's label (2024-2025 counts as 2025), except where that would put two consecutive volumes of a journal in one year and leave the year before empty; then the earlier volume takes the year before, inside its own label. A label that runs into next year counts as its first year. Ten years because, in our October 2026 check, agreement with U.S. News was about the same for any window from eight to twenty years, while shorter windows made the order jumpy: when a five-year window moves on by a year, about four of the top 25 schools move three or more places; with ten years, about one. Any range can be chosen in the filters.

Why doesn't the ranking match U.S. News?

Because it measures something else. The U.S. News overall rank rests mostly on students and outcomes: employment and bar passage carry 58% of its weight, admissions selectivity 10%, and the survey of deans and faculty, the part closest to scholarship, 12.5%. This site counts only where current faculty publish. Even so, the two agree closely: over the top 50 schools, the default view's order has a rank correlation (Spearman) of 0.81 with the U.S. News overall rank and 0.86 with its peer-reputation rank, about as close as the overall rank is to the peer rank itself (0.86). Studies of scholarship-based rankings report 0.62 to 0.80 (for example, Black & Caron, 81 Ind. L.J. 83 (2006)).

Where the two part, it is mostly a matter of how many professors publish. A school's count here tracks the number of its faculty with an article in the selected journals and years closely (a rank correlation of 0.95 over the top 50), so a large, active faculty rises and a small one falls. Of how far a top-50 school's place here differs from its place in U.S. News, about 36% comes from the size of its faculty, 41% from the share of them who published in these journals, and 23% from how many articles each of those published. We checked the obvious alternatives, and none agrees with U.S. News better: counting per professor gives 0.70 to 0.80 over the top 50, depending on who counts as a professor; leaving out articles in a school's own law review (where its faculty publish one and a half to fourteen times as often as other schools' faculty do; a median 19.8% of a host school's count) gives 0.78. Some differences are gaps in the data rather than in scholarship: a few volumes are not collected yet (How recent is the data?), and a professor whose name is written two ways may be split (suggest a correction).

    What counts as a "short piece"?

    "Skip short pieces" leaves out a piece that runs under 10 pages, or a memorial or tribute under 20 pages (where the piece has been identified as one; for now only a few have, so most memorials are judged by the 10-page rule). The length comes from page numbers in HeinOnline's metadata: the next entry's first page minus this one's, counting every entry in the volume's table of contents (a volume's last piece has no next entry, so the classifier decides for it). Lengths are being added volume by volume (). Where a piece's length is not known yet, a trained classifier decides. It learns from the pieces whose length is measured (their titles, the journal, the piece's place in its volume, its co-authors and the titles around it) and is scored by stratified cross-validation on pieces it did not see: . So far: .

    A tribute, dedication, remembrance, roundtable or transcript signed by more than ten people counts as a memorial piece too: it is not a co-authored article. Some table-of-contents entries need care before anyone is credited: an article listed together with its responses ("... (with Responses by ...)") is credited to its own authors, not the respondents; an article continued in a second entry ("(cont.)") counts once; and a piece listed twice in one volume counts once only when it is the same entry (the same page in HeinOnline), not merely the same title on the same printed page of another issue.

    Some examples, all measured:

    • Skipped: a dean's 2-page foreword; a 3-page reply to two other authors in a law review's online companion; a 6-page book review; a 2-page tribute to a judge's twenty years on the bench; 2-page opening remarks at a symposium.
    • Counted: a 12-page response essay; a 13-page essay in an online companion; a 20-page book review; every full article.

    What do the version numbers mean?

    The volume number in the top left is a version, major.data.site. The middle number goes up whenever the substantive data changes (articles, faculty, schools, journals or their ranks; the site keeps a fingerprint of the data to tell), and the small code in the top right is the build: a short hash of the data and the site files together, which changes whenever either does. The last number goes up for changes to the site itself that leave the data alone. The first number goes up for a major update. The version history lists every version.
