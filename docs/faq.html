What is this site?

This site provides rankings and analytics on law school faculty scholarship output. This metric-based sorting of schools helps bring transparency to publications and helps connect communities of scholars. It is modeled on CSrankings.

How are rankings calculated?

Just as they are on CSrankings. Each article is one credit, split evenly between its co-authors (the adjusted count). Every group you select is one area: the flagship law reviews (all the US News tiers you check, together), and each specialty group you check. A school's count is the geometric mean, across the selected areas, of its faculty's total adjusted count in that area plus one. Articles are credited to the school where the author teaches now, so a professor who moves brings their earlier articles along.

What are "flagship" law reviews?

A school's main, general-interest law review, such as the Harvard Law Review or the Northwestern University Law Review. W&L lists these in its "General" category, together with online editions (the Yale Law Journal Forum, for example) and a few other general journals; in the rankings those form their own tier, "Online editions & other general journals", which is off by default. The other journals are specialty journals, which this site groups into thirteen subject areas (plus a group of method journals, "Interdisciplinary Methods"); see Subject areas for the tree, how it was built and its sources. Open a faculty member on the rankings page to see bars for where they publish: one for flagship law reviews, one for online editions & other general journals, and one for each specialty group they have published in, counting all their articles in the years you chose (without short pieces when "Skip short pieces" is on), with how many of them count in the current ranking. The site does not list individual articles; a name links to the person's own faculty page where we have it.

What do the tags next to a name mean?

The subject areas of the specialty journals that person has published in, each with the number of their articles there, over all years, and nothing else. Each specialty journal sits in one field of the subject tree and each field in one area, so the tags are read straight off the journals: no thresholds, and nothing is guessed from titles. Articles in two areas' journals make two tags. Someone who has published only in flagship law reviews (where nearly every law professor publishes, whatever their field) has no tag. Open a faculty member to see "What they work on": the same thing as a small tree (area, field, journals) for the years you chose.

Some examples, as of October 2026: Mark Lemley (Stanford) is tagged Science, Technology & IP 31 (intellectual property 18, technology law & policy 13: the Harvard Journal of Law & Technology, the Berkeley Technology Law Journal and others), and also Constitutional & Public Law 3, Business & Financial Law 2 and Interdisciplinary Methods 1; Richard Revesz (NYU), Property, Environment & Natural Resources 18 (all environmental law, from the Environmental Law Reporter to Ecology Law Quarterly), Constitutional & Public Law 5 and Interdisciplinary Methods 5; Stephen Henderson (Oklahoma), Criminal Law & Justice 9 (criminal procedure), Constitutional & Public Law 2 and Science, Technology & IP 1; Reuven Avi-Yonah (Michigan), Tax, Trusts & Estates 25, International & Comparative Law 9 and four more. A count of 1 is shown like any other: one article in a field's journal is a fact, and the count lets you weigh it. Each of them also publishes in flagship law reviews, which make no tag.

How are articles matched to faculty?

By name, against HeinOnline's lists of faculty by school, where each person appears once under their HeinOnline author name. An article counts for a faculty member when its author, as the journal's table of contents lists it, has the same last and first name, with middle names or initials that do not conflict (the exact name first; then the first and last name when only one person on the lists has it; then the same ignoring a "Jr." or "III" one side leaves off). A name that belongs to two faculty members is credited to neither; an article whose author has a middle initial is not credited to a faculty member listed without one unless at least 85% of the articles under that name that give a middle initial give that one; and in magazines that are not a school's journal (the ABA Journal, for example), an author listed without a middle initial is not credited to a faculty member listed with one, since staff writers share many professors' names. If something looks wrong, please suggest a correction.

How are journals ranked?

  • Flagship law reviews take their school's rank (), and come in tiers of that rank ("1–3", "4–25", "26–50", "51 and above"); schools tied at a rank share a tier, and reviews of unranked schools are in "51 and above". The school rank can be the U.S. News overall rank (the default) or the U.S. News peer-reputation rank, from the scores deans and faculty give each school (as published on TaxProf Blog); choose under "Flagship Law Reviews". The default view counts the main law reviews of the top 25.
  • Specialty journals take their rank in the Washington & Lee Law Journal Rankings (, combined score). They are grouped by subject area and field (see below); open a field in the filters to see its journals in order of W&L combined score, each with its score and the school that publishes it with that school's rank. "Count each group's top N" counts a group's highest-scoring journals (journals W&L does not score count only when "all" is chosen), and "from schools ranked" can limit the specialty journals to those published by schools in a rank range.

These ranks only decide which journals are counted. Schools themselves are ordered by the count described above.

    What counts as a "short piece"?

    "Skip short pieces" leaves out a piece that runs under 10 pages, or a memorial or tribute under 20 pages (where the piece has been identified as one; for now only a few have, so most memorials are judged by the 10-page rule). The length comes from page numbers in HeinOnline's metadata: the next entry's first page minus this one's, counting every entry in the volume's table of contents (a volume's last piece has no next entry, so the classifier decides for it). Lengths are being added volume by volume (). Where a piece's length is not known yet, a trained classifier decides. It learns from the pieces whose length is measured (their titles, the journal, the piece's place in its volume, its co-authors and the titles around it) and is scored by stratified cross-validation on pieces it did not see: .     • Counted: a 12-page response essay; a 13-page essay in an online companion; a 20-page book review; every full article.

    What do the version numbers mean?

    The volume number in the top left is a version, major.data.site. The middle number goes up whenever the substantive data changes (articles, faculty, schools, journals or their ranks; the site keeps a fingerprint of the data to tell), and the timestamp in the top right is the moment of that change, in Unix time. The last number goes up for changes to the site itself that leave the data alone. The first number goes up for a major update. The version history lists every version.
